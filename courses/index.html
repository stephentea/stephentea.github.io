Reviews for courses I took at Carnegie Mellon University.

About

This page contains reviews of courses I took at Carnegie Mellon University between 2022 to 2026. I rate my experience with each course from 1 to 5 stars, with 💖 indicating courses I found amazing (4 stars), and ⭐ indicating courses I found life-changing (5 stars). Note that everything written here is entirely my own, personal experience, and is not reflective of what other students may experience. This page is inspired by numerous other course reviews written by other CMU students.

☀️ Summer 2025

33-142 Physics II

4/5 💖 George Klein

This is the second class in a 2-course sequence on introductory university physics. Topics covered include electric forces, electric fields, Gauss’s law, basic circuits, Lenz’s law, Faraday’s law, Ampere’s law, and culminates with Maxwell’s equations. I took this class online over the summer and found it to be surprisingly enjoyable. Professor Klein is a great lecture who explains concepts clearly and intuitively, and the class is well-run with very clear requirements and assignment due dates.

66-221 Topics of Law: Intellectual Property

3/5 Samir Patel, John Charles Thomas

I took this class over the summer to satisfy one of my CIT General Education Requirements. This class is an introduction to intellectual property law, with topics covered including patents, trademarks, copyright, and trade secret laws (mainly US-based). The workload is extremely light, with only 2 assignments, 1 midterm, and 1 final.

Although the content taught is good to know, I felt the lectures were a bit dry, for instance, reading off the slides directly. In addition, most questions on the homework and exams were doable by just finding the correct case study from the lecture slides, which made them a bit boring.

🌸 Spring 2025

15-410 Operating System Design & Implementation

5/5 ⭐ Dave Eckhardt

15-410 is one of the most famous courses at CMU, known for its difficulty and extensiveness. Students write their own userspace threading and synchronization library, Unix-like kernel for x86, and a basic hypervisor, entirely from scratch (which is a rare experience for most people).

This was one of my favorite classes at CMU (if not my favorite), and I gained a lot of experience in designing robust systems that work under tricky scenarios, organizing complex software projects, and collaborating with a partner in a large codebase built from scratch. Check out my blog post here for a full review, and my project page here for in-depth technical details!

18-447 Computer Architecture

4/5 💖 Scott Mionis, James Hoe

This course is ECE’s first introduction to the wonderful field of computer architecture (follow up courses are 18-740 Modern Computer Architecture and 18-742 Computer Architecture & Systems). Although the instructor is usually Professor James Hoe, this semester was a bit special, with ECE alumni and current Apple engineer Scott Mionis taking his place for the majority of the lectures (all the way from California!).

The course is roughly split into two parts, with the first being CPU design, all the way from single-cycle to an out-of-order, superscalar core with branch prediction (BTB, return address stack, tournament predictor), data forwarding, and stalling. The next part focuses on off-core components, such as cache design, virtual memory, cache coherence, memory consistency models, and multicore chips.

Overall, I really enjoyed the content of the course. Scott is clearly very knowledgeable about this topic, and he ties together concepts from related fields, such as compilers and OS, very well. This course perfectly complements OS (which I was taking concurrently), approaching the same interface from the hardware-side.

Personally, I think everyone interested in systems should study computer architecture, as it allows you to write hardware-aware code (for example, using SIMD, avoiding indirection to minimize branch mispredictions, cache tiling) both for correctness and performance. I think 15-410, 18-447, and 15-418 is the perfect trio of classes for those looking to excel in programming at the software-hardware interface.

The labs are the highlight of this course, as students form teams of 2 to 3, and design a RISC-V CPU from scratch using SystemVerilog throughout the semester, culminating in a competition against other teams under two metrics: MIPS and MIPS/Watt. Our team mainly focused on reducing critical path by changing how we forward data from later stages to earlier stages, and finding the optimal parameters for our L1 cache to maximize hit rate / power ratio. See my project page here for more details!

10-301 Introduction to Machine Learning

5/5 ⭐ Henry Chai, Matt Gormley

10-301 is an amazing first class to machine learning, and achieves its goal of covering a wide breadth of topics without going too much into the mathematical details. If you’re looking for a more rigorous class, consider taking 10-701/10-715 or even a statistics class like 36-700/705.

I was very impressed with how well-organized and structured this course is. Professor Chai and Gormley clearly care a lot about providing the smoothest educational experience and there were almost no hiccups in any of the assignments/ exams (almost like a high-school class).

This course provides a high-level overview into different fields of machine learning, from classical machine learning (decision trees, perceptrons) to deep learning (CNNs, RNNs, Transformers), to theory (fairness, PAC learning), to various other topics (random forests, GANs), with weekly writtens and labs to reinforce lectures. My favorite lab was using Google Colab to train an RNN-LM (on the TinyStories dataset) that generates short fairy tales.

21-301 Combinatorics

3/5 Dylan Altschuler

This course is CMU’s undergraduate offering in the field of combinatorics (graduate courses include 21-737 Probabilistic Combinatorics and 21-738 Extremal Combinatorics). Topics covered include basic counting, graph coloring, Ramsey numbers, Turan numbers, probabilistic methods, Lovasz Local Lemma, Dependent Random Choice, linear algebra methods, spectral graph theory, information theory, and more.

Many proofs were really cool - I was often amazed by how we could prove many strong results from seemingly chaotic, unstructured graphs. My favorite result is the Expander-Mixing Lemma which gives us many insights into graphs, such as edge statistics, chromatic number, and independence number. Also, Paul Erdős shows up many, many times (along with other mathematicians such as Alfréd Rényi, Pál Turán, and Fan Chung).

Although the lectures and homework were of reasonable difficulty, I found the exams to be a bit too easy. Almost every exam problem was taken straight out of review problems or examples from lecture, making exams simply a memorization test. I would have preferred more creative questions that force you to think on the spot, which is one of the reasons I take math classes.

82-119 Arabic Calligraphy

3/5 Ethan Pullman

This class is an introduction to Arabic calligraphy, designed to be accessible for people with no experience with Arabic (like me). The class is well-taught, with weekly homework designed to give you practice in learning the Arabic alphabet/ numbers and practicing Arabic calligraphy in various styles, such as Naskh and Reqa.

My favorite part of this class were the weekly in-class sessions where we practiced Arabic calligraphy with Arabic music playing in the background, and Ustaz Pullman walking around to give each of us personal advice. Unfortunately, due to the high stress of this semester, I could not enjoy the class as much as I would’ve liked to (many times I was thinking about my OS project while writing Arabic letters).

🍁 Fall 2024

18-341 Logic Design & Verification

3/5 Bill Nace

18-341 is ECE’s second course in digital design (after 18-240), with topics covered including advanced SystemVerilog constructs (generate, function, task), interfacing between modules (system rendezvous, lock-step synchronization, handshaking, FIFO queues), clocking for embedded devices (PLL, NRZI), error detection/correction (Hamming codes, CRC), synthesis for FPGAs/ASICs, and testbench constructs (assertions, functional coverage, UVM).

Unfortunately, this course was much less interesting to me compared to 18-240. 18-240 had a guiding thread driving the concepts and labs, starting with combinational logic, then capturing state using FSMs, then scaling up towards datapaths and RTL design. However, 18-341 felt disorganized and unmotivated, with various topics introduced in seemingly arbitrary order, without much connection in between them.

While learning about SystemVerilog is important, I believe too much emphasis was placed on learning the syntax of the language and SystemVerilog-specific details. I prefer learning more about fundamental concepts/principles and design process/ trade-offs, while the syntax of SystemVerilog can be learned individually at the beginning of a project.

In addition, while the projects sound interesting on paper, most were very straightforward (pipelined matrix multiply + addition, Network on Chip), tedious (SystemVerilog assertions), or over-simplified (USB 2.0 Serial Interface Engine). Given that the projects are the highlight of this course, I was a bit disappointed in how lackluster they felt.

15-440 Distributed Systems

4/5 💖 Heather Miller, Wenting Zheng

This class is one of CMU’s core undergraduate/graduate software systems offerings. The course covers many topics that are practical and useful for SWEs, including computer networking, fault tolerance, clock synchronization, RPCs, filesystems, cluster compute, publish-subscribe, virtualization, cryptography, Byzantine Fault Tolerance, and more. I ended up TA-ing for this course!

Note that the Fall and Spring versions of this class significantly differ in both content and structure. My review will be the Fall version, taught in Go. There are 4 homework and 2 exams, each designed to test your knowledge on theory.

The highlight of this course are the 4 projects over the course of the semester (although they can be slightly disconnected from the lectures), which are: Distributed Key-Value Store, Distributed Bitcoin Miner, Raft Consensus Algorithm, and Massively- Multiplayer Online (MMO) Game. See my project page here for a full in-depth dive into the projects!

Overall, this course was quite enjoyable and helped me prepare for systems design interviews for SWE/SDE positions. However, this class felt a bit too interview-preppy, covering many topics in a shallow manner, instead of going deep into selected topics. This is a fair tradeoff though, given that this is the first (and many times, only) systems class that students take.

15-327 Monte Carlo Methods & Applications

4/5 💖 Gautam Iyer, Keenan Crane

After having an amazing experience with 21-325 Probability, I decided to take this course, as it dives deep into various topics in probability very applicable in computer science, especially computer graphics and machine learning: random number generation, sampling, Markov chains, Monte-Carlo integration, and stochastic processes.

I found this course to be very enjoyable. There are weekly homework, which consists of both a written and programming component. Every programming project was extremely interesting, including using MCMC algorithm to sample from the Gibbs distribution, using simulated annealing and tempering to crack a substitution cipher without relying on brute force search, and sampling from different surfaces such as human teeth!

My favorite takeaway from this class is the fundamental tension between randomness and determinism. For instance, integration using samples at fixed intervals is prone to noise, hence people developed Monte-Carlo integration, where points are sampled uniformly randomly. However, people then found that using deterministic, low-discrepancy sequences can generate amazing results as well!

Also, both Professor Iyer and Crane are amazing lecturers! I found Professor Crane’s slides to be beautifully animated, to the point that I seriously considered taking further classes taught by him (such as 15-362 Computer Graphics and 15-458 Discrete Differential Geometry).

15-251 Great Ideas in Theoretical Computer Science

5/5 ⭐ Anil Ada

This course is one of the most well-organized and well-taught courses I have ever taken at CMU. Course content includes DFAs, Turing Machines, Undecidability, Graph Theory, P vs NP, Randomized Algorithms, Cryptography, Quantum Computation, and Communication Complexity, all of which are beautiful and foundational concepts in Theoretical Computer Science.

On top of this, Ada is a phenomenal lecturer who delivers lectures in an engaging way, explains concepts clearly, and writes extremely high-quality notes on his own website PandaNotes.

Despite this, I have a few complaints (most are my fault). Firstly, one of the main objectives of this course is teaching you how to write better proofs. However, I had already taken multiple higher-level math classes (such as 21-355 Real Analysis I, 21-373 Algebraic Structures, and 21-325 Probability), making some content feel a bit too pedagogical and hand-wavy.

Also, many lectures stopped right as we got to the more interesting content, and it felt like we were just scraping the surface of all the topics (which is valid, given that this is a survey class, but I would have liked to cover more topics in-depth, especially related to formal languages and complexity theory). Therefore, I recommend taking this course early (freshman spring or sophomore fall) to get the most out of it, instead of waiting until junior fall like me.

21-373 Algebraic Structures

3/5 Boris Bukh

This was the hardest course I had taken so far at CMU, covering a wide range of topics in abstract algebra, including Group Theory (Lagrange’s Theorem, Isomorphism Theorems, Sylow’s Theorems, etc), Ring Theory (Integral Domains, PIDs, UFDs, Eisenstein’s Criterion, etc), and Field Theory (Splitting Fields, Symmetric Polynomials, Galois Theory, Finite Fields, etc).

I found this course to be extremely challenging, as many new topics were introduced with little motivation, and we got little practice on each topic due to the fast-paced nature of the course. Many proofs were long-winded, and I often had no confidence that I would be able to think of the proofs myself (unlike other courses like 15-251 and 21-355).

With many hours of practice towards the latter half of the course, I noticed my ability to write proofs to significantly improve, in addition to an improved ability to quickly grasp abstract mathematical concepts. Despite its difficulty (often said to be equivalent to 21-235 Math Studies Algebra), this course was rewarding and introduced me to a lot of beautiful ideas in one of the foundational pillars of mathematics.

84-405 Future of Warfare

3/5 Molly Dunigan

This class covers modern warfare in the contemporary environment, taught by Professor Dunigan, who is also a researcher at RAND Corporation. The course investigates new trends in warfare, including AI technology, drones, cyber and information warfare, violent non-state actors, mercenary forces, and gray-zone activities.

I found this to be a standard General Education course - lectures are not super exciting and do not teach groundbreaking material, but they do make you think about warfare in the modern world. There are 3 midterms and a final project, where students form teams of three to five and present a topic of their own choosing.

57-173 Survey of Western Music History

4/5 💖 Paul Johnston

I took this class online over the summer. This class is a sweeping overview of Western music history, starting with Gregorian chants, then moving from Baroque, Classical, Romantic, all the way to Modern music. There is a set of required listenings corresponding to the era introduced each week, along with essays and photo assignments.

As a piano player for 15+ years and a big fan of piano, violin, and orchestral music in general, I found this course to be really enjoyable. As many lectures were discussion based, classes felt like chatting with friends with similar interests, instead of regular lectures. 🌸 Spring 2024

18-240 Digital Systems

5/5 ⭐ Bill Nace

18-240 is a first introduction to digital systems, a core concentration in ECE. The class is divided into three parts: combinational logic, sequential logic, and computer architecture. The first section starts with basic digital gates (AND, OR, NOT, XOR, etc), then quickly dives into canonical SOP/POS form and K-maps. The second section reviews flip-flops (from 18-100) and uses them to design finite-state machines and RTL datapaths. Finally, the third section explores the design process of a basic single-cycle CPU and various design tradeoffs.

I really enjoyed this course, as it dived into the low-level details of hardware and demystified what goes under the hood when running software. For instance, understanding how an FSM works is key to success in later hardware courses such as 18-341 Logic Design and Verification and 18-447 Computer Architecture. I found the topics to be well-motivated and well-structured, with our designs getting more and more powerful as we progressed through the course.

Although the workload (weekly homework + night labs) can be overwhelming at times, they are designed to give you a lot of practice with the hardware workflow, from understanding the specs, designing the RTL, ensuring correctness with simulation using Synopsys VCS, all the way to synthesizing onto an Altera FPGA. Overall, I spent around 15 hours per week on this course.

18-290 Signals & Systems

4/5 💖 Thomas Sullivan, Vijayakumar Bhagavatula

18-290 is one of the four cores in the ECE undergraduate curriculum, and is undoubtedly the most theoretical. The class starts with a brief introduction to various signals (dirac-delta, Kronecker-delta, unit step/ramp) and their properties. The next 10 weeks focus on the central concepts of convolution, CTFS, CTFT, DTFS, and DTFT. The class concludes with real-world implementations (DFT, FFT), applications (solving ODEs, designing filters, sampling/ reconstructing signals), and the Laplace transform.

Although this course is not well-regarded among ECE students, I found it to be surprisingly well-taught and the content to be very interesting. The homework and exams all reinforce content taught in lecture, and I did not experience any of the horror stories that I had heard from previous cohorts.

Signals and systems is a beautiful field in its own right, and taking this course gives you a new perspective into other fields as well (such analyzing RC circuits via Fourier transforms). I seriously considered taking follow-up courses of 18-370 Controls and 18-491 Digital Signal Processing, but ultimately decided against it due to time conflicts with other classes (but I still would love to learn more about signal processing!).

18-310 Semiconductor Devices

3/5 Jimmy Zhu, Xu Zhang

As the name suggests, 18-310 is a theoretical dive into the world of semiconductors that make up our modern world. The class is split into three modules: the first module covers the fundamentals of semiconductors, including doping, energy band diagrams, and drift/ diffusion current. The second module then applies these principles to devices, jumping from PN junction diodes, PIN photodiodes, solar cells, Zener diodes, Schottky diodes to BJTs. The third module is an in-depth analysis of MOSFETs and FLASH memory cells.

The course is well-structured, with weekly homeworks reinforcing concepts taught during lecture and 3 exams in total, one after each module (similar to 18-100). Both Professor Jimmy and Zhang are extremely knowledgeable about the field of semiconductors and explain physical concepts very intuitively.

However, I was not particular excited by this course, as the homework and exams mostly revolved around finding the correct formula and plugging numbers in. Still, this course provides a solid foundation for those interested in nanofabrication and circuit design (many of my friends complemented 18-310 with 18-320 Microelectronic Circuits).

21-325 Probability

5/5 ⭐ Agoston Pisztora

21-325 covers probability theory from the ground up, with the first 2 weeks defining probability spaces using sigma algebras, the next 5 weeks covering discrete probability with topics in combinatorics, branching processes, and random walks, and the remaining 7 weeks covering continuous probability, with topics in absolutely continuous functions, Chebyshev’s inequality, Jensen’s inequality, CLT, wLLN/sLLN, and applications in Brownian motion, Poisson point processes, and queuing theory.

The course provides a rigorous introduction to probability, which is foundational to further studies in probability, statistics, and machine learning. I’ve heard 15-259 Probability and Computing is another great option, but seems to cover less continuous probability, instead placing more emphasis on applications in computer science like Markov chains and randomized algorithms.

This was the course I enjoyed the most this semester. Although the first few weeks were quite repetitive, as most people do combinatorics and basic probability in high school, it quickly moved on to more interesting topics and applications. Professor Pisztora is an amazing lecturer who has a lot of experience in this field (he lectures without referencing any notes, something I’ve never seen before in other math classes!) and gives a lot of motivation and intuition behind theorems.

He also ties together probability with other branches of math, such as using convolutions (which I was learning in 18-290) to compute distributions of sums of random variables, using Jacobians (which I learned in 21-259) to compute transformed densities, using the covariance matrix (which I was learning in 21-241) to compute joint normals, and even using contraction mappings to prove the Central Limit Theorem! Each lecture felt like an adventure, and the class beautifully tied together concepts I thought were unrelated.

Overall, 21-325 is truly an amazing class that I would recommend to anyone who is interested in mathematics and want a rigorous, yet beautiful introduction to probability.

21-241 Matrices & Linear Transformations

3/5 Junichi Koganemaru

This is a first course in linear algebra, covering all of the standard topics: linear systems of equations (Gaussian elimination, RREF, LU-decomposition), vector spaces (subspace, independence, dimension, rank), linear regression (orthogonal subspaces, least-square solutions, QR-decomposition, Gram-Schmidt process), spectral theory (eigenvalues/ eigenvectors/ eigenspace / eigenbasis, trace), linear transformations, and SVD.

Roughly equal emphasis is placed on proofs and computation (in contrast to 21-341 Linear Algebra, which builds upon abstract algebra). I found this to be an average course - nothing too special, but you will walk away with a solid understanding of linear algebra (which I found very helpful later on for courses such as 21-373 Algebraic Structures and 10-301 Machine Learning).

Professor Koganemaru is a new professor at CMU, which made the learning experience a bit rough at times (lectures were a bit disorganized, lecture notes were sometimes missing), but overall he clearly cares a lot about his students, and both the homework and exams were very doable (leaning towards the easy side).

🍁 Fall 2023

18-213 Introduction to Computer Systems

4/5 💖 Greg Kesden, Ziad Youssfi

This class is CMU’s intro-level systems class, covering many important topics such as concurrency, threads, virtual memory, POSIX signals, and socket-based networking. Understanding concepts covered in this class is essential for success in later software systems classes.

Bi-weekly labs form the core experience of this class, with each designed to give you hands-on experience working with core concepts in software systems. These labs have been widely adopted at other universities, including writing a heap-based memory allocator, simple Linux shell, and network proxy including file caching. My favorite lab was malloc!

18-220 Electronic Devices & Analog Circuits

4/5 💖 Mark Budnik, Xu Zhang

This class is one of ECE’s four core courses, and unlocks all future circuits classes such as 18-320 Microelectronic Circuits, 18-622 Digital IC Design, and 18-623 Analog IC Design. This class was extremely well-taught and well-organized, with weekly homework (theory) and night lab sessions (hands-on).

Both Professor Budnik and Zhang explain concepts clearly and step-by-step, and they show a lot of care towards students. Concepts covered include circuit analysis, capacitors, inductors, transformers, and small-signal analysis. My favorite moment was when Professor Budnik dressed up as Gojo from Jujutsu Kaisen for Halloween (he does this every year as a tradition).

15-150 Principles of Functional Programming

4/5 💖 Steve Brookes

This class is one of CS’s six core courses, and is taught entirely in the functional language SML (as the name suggests). Most schools have functional programming as an optional class, which makes CMU unique in this aspect. Course content includes algebraic datatypes, higher-order functions, currying, CPS, work vs span, lazy vs eager evaluation, regular expressions, and more.

At the moment, I did not enjoy this course as much as 15-122, as it can be hard to see how many of the content learned are immediately applicable. However, in the long-run, 15-150 will teach you invaluable skills, allowing you to reason about programming languages in an abstract, mathematical way, and quickly pick up new languages by learning about the common underlying frameworks.

21-355 Principles of Real Analysis I

4/5 💖 Matthew Rosenzweig

This class is one of the core courses in math, and a first introduction to real analysis (follow up course is 21-356 Real Analysis II, but students can alternatively take 21-235 and 21-236 Math Studies Analysis I/II which are much harder). This class closely follows Baby Rudin - a classic undergraduate textbook on real analysis - covering chapters 1 to 7.

Topics covered include point-set topology, sequences, series, continuity, differentiability, and integration. While these sound like topics covered in high school math, real analysis covers them in much more rigor. My favorite result is the Arzelà–Ascoli theorem which characterizes the existence of uniformly convergent subsequences in a closed and bounded interval.

You will never forget the triangle inequality after this class (the Heine-Borel theorem closely follows). I really enjoyed this class and wish to take more analysis courses in the future (such as 21-356 Real Analysis II, 21-623 Complex Analysis, 21-651 General Topology, 21-720 Measure & Integration) if I have space for them.

76-270 Writing for the Professions

2/5 Seth Goldstein

🌸 Spring 2023

15-122 Principles of Imperative Computation

5/5 ⭐ Iliano Cervesato, Dilsun Kaynar

This is the first class out of six CS core courses. Topics covered include standard data structures & algorithms like search algorithms, sorting algorithms, stacks, queues, hash dictionaries, amortized analysis, binary search trees, self-balancing trees, heaps, and graphs. However, unlike similar courses in other universities, emphasis is on proving correctness of code using Hoare logic, such as pre-conditions, loop invariants, and post-conditions.

I found this course to be really enjoyable, treating both the weekly written and programming assignments as fun puzzles. In addition, this course introduced me to C (which eventually became my most used language in college). Understanding the material well in this class is essential for success in later CS courses - for instance, I still reference data structure implementations from 15-122 in advanced CS classes like 15-410.

15-113 Special Topics: Applied Python Programming

3/5 David Kosbie

This is a mini-class (3 units) taught by Professor Kosbie as a follow-up to 15-112. This was the first semester this course was offered, and I believe it is no longer offered anymore.

We met up twice a week, with each session a mixture of Professor Kosbie teaching us about different Python constructs and libraries (such as deque, defaultdict, heap, functools, numpy, scipy, matplotlib, random, context managers, serialization, threads, processes, flask, C extensions) and story time. The last few weeks had students from the class present topics they found interesting.

As this course was being offered for the first time, there were a few rough edges, such as course content for a week not being finalized until the previous week. In addition, as there were no assignments of any kind, a lot of the content flew over my head.

Surprisingly, I ended up remembering much more of the content than I thought I did at the time, which turned out to be useful in many situations. For instance, both 15-327 Monte Carlo Methods and Applications and 10-301 Introduction to Machine Learning expect proficiency in Python (beyond what 15-112 teaches), and I also use Python in computer networking research both at CMU and EPFL.

12-100 Introduction to Civil & Environmental Engineering

2/5 Corey Harper

Students in CIT (College of Engineering) are required to take 2 introduction to engineering courses - presumably to broaden our horizons and help us make an informed decision during declaration day (sometime during freshman spring semester). Hence, I took this class to fulfill this requirement (having already taken 18-100 Introduction to ECE).

This class provides high-level overview of different topics in CEE, including rigid body equilibrium, bending stress, truss theory, basic probability theory, basic queueing theory, and mass balance. There are bi-weekly homework, projects, and exams, but the workload is much lighter than other engineering classes.

Unfortunately, I did not find this class to be enjoyable due to 2 reasons: (1) I was simply not interested in the content, and (2) the lectures were poorly delivered. One of the highlights of this class is forming teams to build a bridge out of wooden sticks, which the professor stands on and tries to break (this may be the only thing I remember from this class…).

21-127 Concepts of Mathematics

5/5 ⭐ Greggo Johnson

This class is CMU’s first introduction to proof-based mathematics, and was my favorite class this semester. Greggo is a charismatic lecturer who explains concepts clearly and rigorously, and both the homework and exams were decently challenging (instead of simply memorizing examples done in class). In fact, this class single-handedly inspired me to pursue a minor in Mathematical Sciences.

Topics covered include propositional logic, basic set theory, induction, relations, cardinality, number theory, and basic counting. The course is organized such that students must pay constant attention, with weekly homework and 3 exams + 1 final. My favorite part of this class were the recitations, led by PhD student Kian Cho who is clearly extremely passionate about mathematics.

21-259 Calculus in Three Dimensions

3/5 Andrew Newman

This is a first couse in multivariate calculus, covering all of the standard topics: quadric surfaces, partial and directional derivatives, Lagrange multiplier, line and surface integrals, Green-Gauss Theorem, and Stokes’ Theorem. There are weekly homework and 3 midterms + 1 final. This class is representative of the workload of a typical math class for engineers (instead of upper-level math classes for math majors).

Many ECE students take 21-259 + 21-241 to satisfy their math requirements (instead of 18-202, which I have not heard many good things about). I did not find this course particularly exciting, but found the content learned to be surprisingly applicable to future courses such as 21-325 Probability, 15-327 Monte Carlo Methods and Applications, and especially 33-142 Physics II.

76-106 Writing about Data

3/5 Elizabeth Dieterich

This is a mini-course (4.5 units) that students can take to fulfill their first-year English requirement (which is required of every student at CMU). I found this class to be quite dry, as most of the content seemed obvious to me, such as how to effectively use graphs in written text. Luckily, this class was easy and didn’t take up much time.

76-107 Writing about Literature

3/5 Don Holmes

This is the second mini-course I took to fulfill my first-year English requirement. Note that the topic for this class depends heavily on which professor is teaching it. For instance, our class focused on Afrofuturism, as this was Professor Holmes’ specialty. I found this class to be similar to a standard high school English class, where we read books / watch videos and analyze them in our essays.

18-100 Introduction to Electrical & Computer Engineering

5/5 ⭐ Jimmy Zhu

This class is an introduction to ECE, taught by Professor Jimmy Zhu, who everyone in ECE loves (we gave applauses after every lecture, which I never saw in any other class afterwards). Jimmy covers basic topics in the various fields within ECE, including digital circuits, flip-flops, capacitors, inductors, transformers, signal processing, computer networking, and even machine learning (!).

My favorite part of this class is undoubtedly its labs. All students were given a toolbox containing breadboards, wires, different circuit components, screws, drivers, and more, which we used to complete our labs. We built a 555-timer, radio receiver/ transmitter pair, and more, all from scratch. I also met many of my best friends through these labs, all of whom I continued taking classes with in higher-level ECE courses.

15-112 Fundamentals of Computer Science & Programming

4/5 💖 David Kosbie

This is a famous course in CMU, designed to bring students with no experience in programming all the way from scratch to being able to build small-sized projects in Python entirely on their own. As I had no formal programming background prior to college (I only did programming in Python for fun), I decided to start with this course instead of the more advanced 15-122.

I found the topics covered to be a bit too easy, and the content only got slightly more interesting/ challenging towards the end with the introduction of recursion and backtracking. For my term project, I coded a Python script that uses OpenCV to parse Sudoku boards on the New York Times website, uses backtracking and several other optimization techniques to solve the Sudoku board, then fills the board automatically using the Selenium web driver.

My favorite aspect of this course was Professor David Kosbie, who has a strong sense of humor. I enjoyed the talks he gave during the beginning/ end of each class, where he would give us insights into the industry and his own experiences as a computer scientist. In fact, I liked Kosbie so much that I took the follow-up course 15-113 (which was offered for the first time).

21-122 Integration & Approximation

3/5 Alan Lew

This is CMU’s second course in calculus, covering different techniques in integration, using different tests to determine convergence/ divergence of series, and different approximations for integrals.

Although the course itself is structured fine, I highly regret taking this, as I already had credit from IB classes in high school, but willingly decided to not take these credits as I was scared by the difficulty of CMU that I had read about online. These worries turned out to be unfounded, and 21-122 ended up being one of the easiest courses I took at CMU (other than GenEds).

Professor Lew is a really sweet professor who genuinely cares about the success of his students, and uses beautifully-animated slides to explain the material. As I later found out, he specializes in combinatorics, as my professor for 21-301 heavily referenced his material.

70-100 Global Business

3/5 Serra Boranbay Akan

This class is an introduction to Business Administration for non-majors, covering a wide range of topics in business, including discount factor, labor markets, stock markets, bonds, interest rates, supply chain management, and more. The highlight of this class is a group project, where students form teams of 4, come up with a business idea, and present it in front of the entire class.

I largely enjoyed the content of this class, and Professor Boranbay is one of the most caring professors I’ve had at CMU (she tries to get to know every student personally). However, the group project felt a bit lackluster and underwhelming due to a combination of: (1) unclear project requirements, (2) lenient grading and feedback, and (3) lack of collaboration and communication between group members.

Overall, I would recommend this class for students looking for an easy class to fulfill their General Education requirements (I spent around 5 hours a week on this class), or for those pursuing a minor in Business Administration (which I considered seriously during freshman year, but eventually decided against in order to prioritize other classes).
