with 💖 indicating courses I found amazing (4 stars), and ⭐ indicating courses I 
found life-changing (5 stars). Note that everything written here is entirely my own, 
personal experience, and is not reflective of what other students may experience. 
This page is inspired by numerous other course reviews written by other CMU students.

## ☀️ Summer 2025
### 33-142 Physics II
**4/5 💖** *George Klein*

This is the second class in a 2-course sequence on introductory university physics. 
Topics covered include electric forces, electric fields, Gauss's law, basic circuits, 
Lenz's law, Faraday's law, Ampere's law, and culminates with Maxwell's equations. 
I took this class online over the summer and found it to be surprisingly enjoyable. 
Professor Klein is a great lecture who explains concepts clearly and intuitively, 
and the class is well-run with very clear requirements and assignment due dates. 

### 66-221 Topics of Law: Intellectual Property
**3/5** *Samir Patel, John Charles Thomas*

I took this class over the summer to satisfy one of my CIT General Education 
Requirements. This class is an introduction to intellectual property law, with 
topics covered including [patents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patent), [trademarks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trademark), [copyright](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copyright), and [trade secret laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trade_secret) (mainly US-based). The workload is extremely light, with only 2 assignments, 1 midterm, and 1 final. 

Although the content taught is good to know, I felt the lectures were a bit dry, 
for instance, reading off the slides directly. In addition, most questions on the 
homework and exams were doable by just finding the correct case study from the 
lecture slides, which made them a bit boring. 


## 🌸 Spring 2025
### 15-410 Operating System Design & Implementation
**5/5 ⭐** *Dave Eckhardt*

15-410 is one of the most famous courses at CMU, known for its difficulty and 
extensiveness. Students write their own userspace threading and synchronization 
library, Unix-like kernel for x86, and a basic hypervisor, entirely from scratch 
(which is a rare experience for most people).

This was one of my favorite classes at CMU (if not my favorite), and I gained a 
lot of experience in designing robust systems that work under tricky scenarios, 
organizing complex software projects, and collaborating with a partner in a large 
codebase built from scratch.  Check out my blog post [here]() for a full review, 
and my project page [here](../projects/1_project) for in-depth technical details!

### 18-447 Computer Architecture
**4/5 💖** *Scott Mionis, James Hoe*

This course is ECE's first introduction to the wonderful field of computer 
architecture (follow up courses are 18-740 Modern Computer Architecture and 
18-742 Computer Architecture & Systems). Although the instructor is usually 
Professor James Hoe, this semester was a bit special, with ECE alumni and current 
Apple engineer [Scott Mionis](https://www.linkedin.com/in/scott-mionis-655196127/) 
taking his place for the majority of the lectures (all the way from California!).

The course is roughly split into two parts, with the first being CPU design, 
all the way from single-cycle to an [out-of-order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Out-of-order_execution), [superscalar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superscalar_processor) core with branch 
prediction ([BTB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Branch_target_predictor), [return address stack](https://www.usenix.org/legacy/events/sec01/full_papers/frantzen/frantzen_html/node11.html), [tournament predictor](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/afs/cs/academic/class/15740-f18/www/lectures/16-branch-prediction.pdf)), [data forwarding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operand_forwarding), and [stalling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pipeline_stall). The next part focuses on off-core components, such as cache design, virtual memory, 
[cache coherence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cache_coherence), [memory consistency models](https://jamesbornholt.com/blog/memory-models/), and [multicore chips](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multi-core_processor). 

Overall, I really enjoyed the content of the course. Scott is clearly very 
knowledgeable about this topic, and he ties together concepts from related fields, 
such as compilers and OS, very well.  This course perfectly complements OS (which I 
was taking concurrently), approaching the same interface from the hardware-side. 

Personally, I think everyone interested in systems should study computer architecture, 
as it allows you to write hardware-aware code (for example, using [SIMD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single_instruction,_multiple_data), avoiding indirection to minimize branch mispredictions, [cache tiling](https://www.intel.com/content/www/us/en/developer/articles/technical/efficient-use-of-tiling.html)) both for correctness and performance. I think 15-410, 18-447, and [15-418](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~15418/) is the perfect trio of classes for those looking to excel in programming at the software-hardware interface.

The labs are the highlight of this course, as students form teams of 2 to 3, and 
design a [RISC-V](https://riscv.org/) CPU from scratch using SystemVerilog throughout the semester, culminating in a competition against other teams under two metrics: [MIPS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instructions_per_second#Millions_of_instructions_per_second_(MIPS)) and MIPS/Watt. Our team 
mainly focused on reducing critical path by changing how we forward data from later 
stages to earlier stages, and finding the optimal parameters for our L1 cache to 
maximize `hit rate / power` ratio. See my project page [here](../projects/2_project) for more details!

### 10-301 Introduction to Machine Learning
**5/5 ⭐** *Henry Chai, Matt Gormley*

10-301 is an amazing first class to machine learning, and achieves its goal of 
covering a wide breadth of topics without going too much into the mathematical details. 
If you’re looking for a more rigorous class, consider taking 10-701/10-715 or even a 
statistics class like 36-700/705.  

I was very impressed with how well-organized and structured this course is. Professor 
Chai and Gormley clearly care a lot about providing the smoothest 
educational experience and there were almost no hiccups in any of the assignments/
exams (almost like a high-school class).

This course provides a high-level overview into different fields of machine 
learning, from classical machine learning ([decision trees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decision_tree), [perceptrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perceptron)) to 
deep learning ([CNNs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convolutional_neural_network), [RNNs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recurrent_neural_network), [Transformers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transformer_(deep_learning_architecture))), to theory ([fairness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fairness_(machine_learning)), [PAC learning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probably_approximately_correct_learning)), 
to various other topics ([random forests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_forest), [GANs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generative_adversarial_network)), with weekly writtens and labs 
to reinforce lectures. My favorite lab was using Google Colab to train an RNN-LM 
(on the [TinyStories dataset](https://huggingface.co/datasets/roneneldan/TinyStories)) that generates short fairy tales.

### 21-301 Combinatorics
**3/5** *Dylan Altschuler*

This course is CMU's undergraduate offering in the field of combinatorics 
(graduate courses include 21-737 Probabilistic Combinatorics and 21-738 Extremal 
Combinatorics). Topics covered include basic counting, [graph coloring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graph_coloring), [Ramsey numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramsey%27s_theorem), [Turan numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tur%C3%A1n_number), [probabilistic methods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probabilistic_method), [Lovasz Local Lemma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lov%C3%A1sz_local_lemma), 
[Dependent Random Choice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dependent_random_choice), [linear algebra methods](https://people.cs.uchicago.edu/~laci/babai-frankl-book2022.pdf), [spectral graph theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectral_graph_theory), [information theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory), and more.

Many proofs were really cool - I was often amazed by how we could prove many 
strong results from seemingly chaotic, unstructured graphs. My favorite result 
is the [Expander-Mixing Lemma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expander_mixing_lemma) 
which gives us many insights into graphs, such as edge statistics, chromatic number, 
and independence number. Also, [Paul Erdős](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Erd%C5%91s) 
shows up many, many times (along with other mathematicians such as [Alfréd Rényi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfr%C3%A9d_R%C3%A9nyi), [Pál Turán](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P%C3%A1l_Tur%C3%A1n), and 
[Fan Chung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fan_Chung)).

Although the lectures and homework were of reasonable difficulty, I found 
the exams to be a bit too easy.  Almost every exam problem was taken straight 
out of review problems or examples from lecture, making exams simply a memorization 
test. I would have preferred more creative questions that force you to think on 
the spot, which is one of the reasons I take math classes. 

### 82-119 Arabic Calligraphy
**3/5** *Ethan Pullman*

This class is an introduction to Arabic calligraphy, designed to be accessible 
for people with no experience with Arabic (like me). The class is well-taught, 
with weekly homework designed to give you practice in learning the Arabic alphabet/ 
numbers and practicing Arabic calligraphy in various styles, such as [Naskh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naskh_(script)) and [Reqa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reqa%27).

My favorite part of this class were the weekly in-class sessions where we practiced 
Arabic calligraphy with Arabic music playing in the background, and Ustaz Pullman 
walking around to give each of us personal advice. Unfortunately, due to the high 
stress of this semester, I could not enjoy the class as much as I would've liked to 
(many times I was thinking about my OS project while writing Arabic letters).

## 🍁 Fall 2024
### 18-341 Logic Design & Verification
**3/5** *Bill Nace*

18-341 is ECE's second course in digital design (after 18-240), with topics covered including 
advanced SystemVerilog constructs (`generate`, `function`, `task`), interfacing between modules 
(system rendezvous, [lock-step synchronization, handshaking](https://www.ni.com/docs/en-US/bundle/ni-daqmx/page/synchrontypes.html?srsltid=AfmBOoogXGv5OO_s2cqUGPImhUVeosJcy3wm0V9cNXDJ8EcqXOrxCg4q), FIFO queues), clocking for embedded devices ([PLL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase-locked_loop), [NRZI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-return-to-zero)), error detection/correction ([Hamming codes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamming_code), [CRC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclic_redundancy_check)), synthesis for 
FPGAs/ASICs, and testbench constructs ([assertions](https://www.chipverify.com/systemverilog/systemverilog-assertions), [functional coverage](https://www.chipverify.com/systemverilog/systemverilog-functional-coverage), [UVM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_Verification_Methodology)).

Unfortunately, this course was much less interesting to me compared to 18-240. 
18-240 had a guiding thread driving the concepts and labs, starting with 
combinational logic, then capturing state using [FSMs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite-state_machine), then scaling up towards datapaths and [RTL design](https://www.ansys.com/simulation-topics/what-is-register-transfer-level-design).  However, 18-341 felt disorganized and 
unmotivated, with various topics introduced in seemingly arbitrary order, without 
much connection in between them. 

While learning about SystemVerilog is important, I believe too much emphasis was 
placed on learning the syntax of the language and SystemVerilog-specific details. 
I prefer learning more about fundamental concepts/principles and design process/ 
trade-offs, while the syntax of SystemVerilog can be learned individually at the 
beginning of a project.

In addition, while the projects sound interesting on paper, most were very straightforward 
([pipelined](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pipeline_(computing)) matrix multiply + addition, [Network on Chip](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Network_on_a_chip)), tedious (SystemVerilog assertions), 
or over-simplified (USB 2.0 Serial Interface Engine). Given that the projects are the highlight of this 
course, I was a bit disappointed in how lackluster they felt.

### 15-440 Distributed Systems
**4/5 💖** *Heather Miller, Wenting Zheng*

This class is one of CMU's core undergraduate/graduate software systems offerings. 
The course covers many topics that are practical and useful for SWEs, including 
computer networking, fault tolerance, clock synchronization, RPCs, filesystems, 
cluster compute, publish-subscribe, virtualization, cryptography, Byzantine 
Fault Tolerance, and more. I ended up [TA-ing](../teaching) for this course!

Note that the Fall and Spring versions of this class significantly differ in both 
content and structure. My review will be the Fall version, taught in [Go](https://go.dev/). 
There are 4 homework and 2 exams, each designed to test your knowledge on theory. 

The highlight of this course are the 4 projects over the course of the semester 
(although they can be slightly disconnected from the lectures), which are: Distributed 
Key-Value Store, Distributed Bitcoin Miner, Raft Consensus Algorithm, and Massively- 
Multiplayer Online (MMO) Game.  See my project page [here](../projects/3_project) 
for a full in-depth dive into the projects!

Overall, this course was quite enjoyable and helped me prepare for systems 
design interviews for SWE/SDE positions. However, this class felt a bit too 
interview-preppy, covering many topics in a shallow manner, instead of going 
deep into selected topics. This is a fair tradeoff though, given that this is the 
first (and many times, only) systems class that students take. 

### 15-327 Monte Carlo Methods & Applications
**4/5 💖** *Gautam Iyer, Keenan Crane*

After having an amazing experience with [21-325 Probability](#21-325-probability), 
I decided to take this course, as it dives deep into various topics in probability 
very applicable in computer science, especially computer graphics and machine learning: 
[random number generation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_number_generation), sampling, [Markov chains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Markov_chain), [Monte-Carlo integration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_integration), and [stochastic processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stochastic_process). 

I found this course to be very enjoyable. There are weekly homework, which consists 
of both a written and programming component. Every programming project was extremely 
interesting, including using [MCMC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Markov_chain_Monte_Carlo) algorithm to sample from the [Gibbs distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gibbs_measure), 
using [simulated annealing and tempering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simulated_annealing) to crack a substitution cipher without relying on brute force search, and sampling from different 
surfaces such as human teeth!

My favorite takeaway from this class is the fundamental tension between randomness 
and determinism. For instance, integration using samples at fixed intervals is prone 
to noise, hence people developed Monte-Carlo integration, where points are sampled 
uniformly randomly. However, people then found that using deterministic, [low-discrepancy sequences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low-discrepancy_sequence) can generate amazing results as well!

Also, both Professor Iyer and Crane are amazing lecturers! I found Professor Crane's 
slides to be *beautifully* animated, to the point that I seriously considered taking 
further classes taught by him (such as 15-362 Computer Graphics and 15-458 Discrete 
Differential Geometry).


### 15-251 Great Ideas in Theoretical Computer Science
**5/5 ⭐** *Anil Ada*

This course is one of the most well-organized and well-taught courses I have 
ever taken at CMU.  Course content includes [DFAs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deterministic_finite_automaton), [Turing Machines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turing_machine), [Undecidability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undecidable_problem), [Graph Theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graph_theory), [P vs NP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P_versus_NP_problem), [Randomized
Algorithms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Randomized_algorithm), [Cryptography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptography), [Quantum Computation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_computing), and [Communication Complexity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communication_complexity), all of which are
beautiful and foundational concepts in Theoretical Computer Science.


On top of this, Ada is a phenomenal lecturer who delivers lectures in an engaging 
way, explains concepts clearly, and writes extremely high-quality notes on his 
own website PandaNotes.


Despite this, I have a few complaints (most are my fault). Firstly, one of the main 
objectives of this course is teaching you how to write better proofs. However, 
I had already taken multiple higher-level math classes (such as [21-355 Real Analysis I](#21-355-principles-of-real-analysis-i), [21-373 Algebraic Structures](#21-373-algebraic-structures), and [21-325 Probability](#21-325-probability)), making some content feel a bit 
too pedagogical and hand-wavy.


Also, many lectures stopped right as we got to the more interesting content, 
and it felt like we were just scraping the surface of all the topics (which is 
valid, given that this is a survey class, but I would have liked to cover more
topics in-depth, especially related to formal languages and complexity theory).
Therefore, I recommend taking this course early (freshman spring or sophomore fall) 
to get the most out of it, instead of waiting until junior fall like me.


### 21-373 Algebraic Structures
**3/5** *Boris Bukh*

This was the hardest course I had taken so far at CMU, covering a wide range
of topics in abstract algebra, including Group Theory ([Lagrange's Theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrange%27s_theorem_(group_theory)), 
[Isomorphism Theorems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphism_theorems), [Sylow's Theorems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sylow_theorems), etc), Ring Theory ([Integral Domains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integral_domain),
[PIDs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principal_ideal_domain), [UFDs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unique_factorization_domain), [Eisenstein's Criterion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eisenstein%27s_criterion), etc), and Field Theory ([Splitting Fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Splitting_field), 
[Symmetric Polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetric_polynomial), [Galois Theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galois_theory), [Finite Fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_field), etc).


I found this course to be extremely challenging, as many new topics were
introduced with little motivation, and we got little practice on each topic
due to the fast-paced nature of the course. Many proofs were long-winded, and
I often had no confidence that I would be able to think of the proofs myself 
(unlike other courses like 15-251 and 21-355).


With many hours of practice towards the latter half of the course, I noticed
my ability to write proofs to significantly improve, in addition to an improved
ability to quickly grasp abstract mathematical concepts. Despite its difficulty 
(often said to be equivalent to 21-235 Math Studies Algebra), this course was
rewarding and introduced me to a lot of beautiful ideas in one of the foundational
pillars of mathematics.

## 🌸 Spring 2024
### 18-240 Digital Systems
**5/5 ⭐** *Bill Nace*

18-240 is a first introduction to digital systems, a core concentration in ECE. 
The class is divided into three parts: combinational logic, sequential logic, and computer architecture. 
The first section starts with basic digital gates (AND, OR, NOT, XOR, etc), then quickly dives into canonical [SOP/POS form](https://www.tutorialspoint.com/digital-electronics/logical-expression-in-sop-and-pos-form.htm) and [K-maps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karnaugh_map). The second section reviews [flip-flops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flip-flop_(electronics)) (from 18-100) and uses them to design [finite-state machines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite-state_machine) and [RTL datapaths](https://www.ansys.com/simulation-topics/what-is-register-transfer-level-design). Finally, the third section explores the design process of a basic single-cycle CPU and various design tradeoffs.

I really enjoyed this course, as it dived into the low-level details of hardware 
and demystified what goes under the hood when running software. For instance, understanding 
how an FSM works is key to success in later hardware courses such as [18-341 Logic Design and Verification](#18-341-logic-design--verification) and [18-447 Computer Architecture](#18-447-computer-architecture). I found the topics to be well-motivated and well-structured, with our designs getting more and more 
powerful as we progressed through the course. 

Although the workload (weekly homework + night labs) can be overwhelming at times, 
they are designed to give you a lot of practice with the hardware workflow, from 
understanding the specs, designing the RTL, ensuring correctness with simulation 
using [Synopsys VCS](https://www.synopsys.com/verification/simulation/vcs.html), 
all the way to synthesizing onto an [Altera FPGA](https://www.altera.com/). 
Overall, I spent around 15 hours per week on this course.

### 18-290 Signals & Systems
**4/5 💖** *Thomas Sullivan, Vijayakumar Bhagavatula*

18-290 is one of the four cores in the ECE undergraduate curriculum, and is 
undoubtedly the most theoretical.  The class starts with a brief introduction 
to various signals ([dirac-delta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirac_delta_function), [Kronecker-delta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kronecker_delta), [unit step/ramp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramp_function)) and their properties. The next 10 weeks focus on the central concepts of convolution, [CTFS](https://eng.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Electrical_Engineering/Signal_Processing_and_Modeling/Signals_and_Systems_(Baraniuk_et_al.)/06%3A_Continuous_Time_Fourier_Series_(CTFS)/6.02%3A_Continuous_Time_Fourier_Series_(CTFS)), [CTFT](https://eng.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Electrical_Engineering/Signal_Processing_and_Modeling/Signals_and_Systems_(Baraniuk_et_al.)/08%3A_Continuous_Time_Fourier_Transform_(CTFT)/8.02%3A_Continuous_Time_Fourier_Transform_(CTFT)), [DTFS](https://eng.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Electrical_Engineering/Signal_Processing_and_Modeling/Signals_and_Systems_(Baraniuk_et_al.)/07%3A_Discrete_Time_Fourier_Series_(DTFS)/7.02%3A_Discrete_Time_Fourier_Series_(DTFS)), and [DTFT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete-time_Fourier_transform). The class concludes with real-world implementations ([DFT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_Fourier_transform), [FFT)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fast_Fourier_transform), applications (solving ODEs, designing filters, sampling/ reconstructing signals), and the [Laplace transform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laplace_transform).

Although this course is not well-regarded among ECE students, I found it to be
surprisingly well-taught and the content to be very interesting. The homework and 
exams all reinforce content taught in lecture, and I did not experience any of the
horror stories that I had heard from previous cohorts.

Signals and systems is a beautiful field in its own right, and taking this 
course gives you a new perspective into other fields as well (such analyzing 
RC circuits via Fourier transforms).  I seriously considered taking follow-up 
courses of 18-370 Controls and 18-491 Digital Signal Processing, but ultimately
decided against it due to time conflicts with other classes (but I still would 
love to learn more about signal processing!).


### 18-310 Semiconductor Devices
**3/5** *Jimmy Zhu, Xu Zhang*

As the name suggests, 18-310 is a theoretical dive into the world of semiconductors 
that make up our modern world.  The class is split into three modules: the first module covers the fundamentals of semiconductors, including [doping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doping_(semiconductor)), [energy band diagrams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Band_diagram), and drift/ diffusion current.  The second module then applies these principles to devices, jumping from [PN junction diodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P%E2%80%93n_diode), [PIN photodiodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PIN_diode), [solar cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_cell), [Zener diodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zener_diode), [Schottky diodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schottky_diode) to [BJTs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bipolar_junction_transistor).  The third module is an in-depth analysis of [MOSFETs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MOSFET) and [FLASH memory cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flash_memory).


The course is well-structured, with weekly homeworks reinforcing concepts taught
during lecture and 3 exams in total, one after each module (similar to 18-100).
Both Professor Jimmy and Zhang are extremely knowledgeable about the field of 
semiconductors and explain physical concepts very intuitively. 

However, I was not particular excited by this course, as the homework and exams 
mostly revolved around finding the correct formula and plugging numbers in. Still,
this course provides a solid foundation for those interested in nanofabrication and 
circuit design (many of my friends complemented 18-310 with 18-320 Microelectronic
Circuits).

### 21-325 Probability
**5/5 ⭐** *Agoston Pisztora*

21-325 covers probability theory from the ground up, with the first 2 weeks defining
probability spaces using [sigma algebras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%CE%A3-algebra), 
the next 5 weeks covering discrete probability with topics in combinatorics, 
[branching processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Branching_process), and 
[random walks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_walk), and the remaining
7 weeks covering continuous probability, with topics in [absolutely continuous functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolute_continuity), [Chebyshev’s inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chebyshev%27s_inequality), [Jensen’s inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jensen%27s_inequality), [CLT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_limit_theorem), [wLLN/sLLN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_large_numbers), and applications in [Brownian motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brownian_motion), 
[Poisson point processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poisson_point_process), and [queuing theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queueing_theory).

The course provides a rigorous introduction to probability, which is foundational 
to further studies in probability, statistics, and machine learning. I've heard 
15-259 Probability and Computing is another great option, but seems to cover less 
continuous probability, instead placing more emphasis on applications in computer
science like Markov chains and randomized algorithms.

This was the course I enjoyed the most this semester. Although the first few weeks 
were quite repetitive, as most people do combinatorics and basic probability in 
high school, it quickly moved on to more interesting topics and applications. 
Professor Pisztora is an amazing lecturer who has a lot of experience in this field 
(he lectures without referencing any notes, something I’ve never seen before in other 
math classes!) and gives a lot of motivation and intuition behind theorems.  

He also ties together probability with other branches of math, such as using 
[convolutions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convolution) (which I was learning in 18-290) to compute distributions of sums of random variables, using [Jacobians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobian_matrix_and_determinant) (which I learned in 21-259) to compute 
transformed densities, using the covariance matrix (which I was learning in 21-241) 
to compute [joint normals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multivariate_normal_distribution), and even using [contraction mappings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contraction_mapping) to prove the Central Limit Theorem!  Each lecture felt like an adventure, and the class beautifully tied together concepts I thought were unrelated.

Overall, 21-325 is truly an amazing class that I would recommend to anyone who 
is interested in mathematics and want a rigorous, yet beautiful introduction 
to probability. 


### 21-241 Matrices & Linear Transformations
**3/5** *Junichi Koganemaru*

This is a first course in linear algebra, covering all of the standard topics: 
linear systems of equations ([Gaussian elimination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_elimination), [RREF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Row_echelon_form), [LU-decomposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LU_decomposition)), vector spaces (subspace, [independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_independence), [dimension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimension_(vector_space)), [rank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rank_(linear_algebra))), linear regression (orthogonal 
subspaces, [least-square solutions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Least_squares), [QR-decomposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/QR_decomposition), [Gram-Schmidt process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gram%E2%80%93Schmidt_process)), 
spectral theory (eigenvalues/ eigenvectors/ eigenspace / eigenbasis, [trace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trace_(linear_algebra))), 
[linear transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_map), and [SVD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singular_value_decomposition). 

Roughly equal emphasis is placed on proofs and computation (in contrast to 21-341

Professor Koganemaru is a new professor at CMU, which made the learning 
experience a bit rough at times (lectures were a bit disorganized, lecture notes were sometimes 
missing), but overall he clearly cares a lot about his students, and both the homework 
and exams were very doable (leaning towards the easy side).

### 21-355 Principles of Real Analysis I
**4/5 💖** *Matthew Rosenzweig*

This class is one of the core courses in math, and a first introduction to [real analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_analysis) (follow up course is 21-356 Real Analysis II, but students can alternatively take 21-235 and 21-236 Math Studies Analysis I/II which are much harder).  This class closely follows

Topics covered include [point-set topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_topology),

I really enjoyed this class and wish to take more analysis courses in the future
